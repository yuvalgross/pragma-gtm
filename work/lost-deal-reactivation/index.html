Work sample

Lost deal reactivation: which of 760 lost accounts are worth a second call.

A weekly play for an SMB software company. It reads a HubSpot export of every account that did not buy, decides which are worth another try now, who calls them and with what reason, and hands each of five sellers and the appointment setting team a short list every Monday.

The answer

175 of the 760 accounts are worth a second try now. Each has a reason to talk: 78 have a competitor contract ending or a deal that stalled on timing, and 97 showed interest this year. Half of the 175 have had no contact for 11 months.

Who calls. An account that saw a demo goes to a seller. An account that never saw one goes to appointment setting. Never both. Accounts owned by people no longer on the sales team go to the seller with the shortest list.

What counts as a reason to talk

  • A competitor contract that ends within 6 months. 32 accounts.
  • A loss on timing or no answer. 46 accounts.
  • Interest this year: pricing page, case study, webinar or event stand. 112 accounts.

The first two rank ahead of the third. Inside a group, accounts where the team knows the owner or managing director go first, then core ICP, then the best lead sources.

What is cut. Accounts outside the target markets, key accounts with their own seller, and current customers. 64 have a clear no on file: no opportunity, not ICP, too small or a chain. Nothing outweighs a clear no, not even a contract ending. Size cuts nobody.

Four rules I chose

Each is one setting. Change it, run again, and the list shows what moved.

  • Wait 90 days after a loss. Of 67 accounts lost in the first months of the year, 20 came back to the marketing on their own, only 1 within 30 days, and half took 98 days or more.
  • A 6 month window for competitor contracts. An account decides on renewal before the end date. Six months is my judgement; there are too few contract dates to test it yet.
  • A newsletter click alone is not a reason to call. It shows the account reads the emails, not that it is buying. It only breaks a tie.
  • A person reviews the 38 probable duplicates. The match is on name, country and size because the export has no website field. That is not proof, and a wrong merge could put a customer on a call list.

The prototype

One Python script reads the export and writes this week's top 40: 5 accounts for each seller and 15 for appointment setting, each with the reason, a suggested angle and the caller. All 40 have a contract or timing reason, and every contact is an owner or managing director. Same input gives the same output, with no AI call or paid tool inside the run. A second week run gives 40 new accounts with no repeats.

It also runs as a Claude skill: attach the export and ask for the reactivation play. A dashboard page shows the management view, each seller's calls, all 760 accounts with search, and a Decisions tab where a rule can be changed to see the effect at once.

Nine checks on every run. The run fails loudly if any breaks: one decision per account, no account on two lists, no seller over 5, appointment setting at most 15, no excluded market, key account or customer in the top 40, no clear no, seller accounts saw a demo and setting accounts did not, and no opted out or invalid contact on the email list.

Running it every week in HubSpot

Every Monday the ranking runs on a fresh export and writes its result to each account through a private app. If a run fails or a check breaks, nothing is written and last week's lists stay.

  1. Hand out the week. Sets the owner and creates one call task with reason, angle and fit score.
  2. German sequence. For accounts whose competitor contract is ending, the seller calls first and three German mails follow. It stops on a reply or a booked demo.
  3. Email first. One mail. A click on pricing or the case study moves the account to call first.
  4. End of wait. After 90 days, or 6 months before a contract ends, the account is ranked again.
  5. Ask the seller. One task per unqualified account: still unqualified, yes or no, and why.
  6. Demo booked. Creates the deal, stamps the source and hands a setter's account to a seller.
  7. Stop. A new deal, a new customer or an opt out removes the account from every list.

Clean data. The lost reason was empty on 242 of 460 lost deals, so it becomes a required dropdown with a clear no as a value. The website domain becomes required so HubSpot catches most new duplicates.

The low case uses the team's own rate of 1 demo per 10 accounts reached. The high case assumes 1 in 5 for accounts that already saw a demo (assumed). Both are generous: the win rates come from first time demos, and most of these accounts already said no once.

How I will know. After four weeks the sellers have called the first 92 accounts. If fewer than 1 in 10 took a demo, I stop and the 102 are not called. If 1 in 5 did, the high numbers hold.
